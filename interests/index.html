About

My background and education.

I’m CTO and co-founder of Unsigned Research, a systematic trading firm. I build and deploy production data pipelines, statistical & machine learning forecasting models and agentic AI systems, using rigorous scientific principles.

I was previously an academic working in mathematics and biophysics. As a Herchel Smith Research Fellow at the University of Cambridge, I studied the theory of active matter and built models of how living tissues take shape. I worked in close collaboration with experimental biologists, using experimental data to inform models and make new predictions.

Current work

What I do at Unsigned Research.

At Unsigned Research I lead research and engineering. Here are some of the things we do:

  • Time-series forecasting. We build features from noisy, low-signal data, using statistical methods to identify their predictive power. We integrate factors with signal spanning our existing library into our proprietary machine learning models.
  • Risk management. We model and measure risk using factor models and the theory of active portfolio management, creating predictive forecasts to construct our portfolio and explanatory decompositions of realised outcomes.
  • Production infra. We run a scalable cloud-native infrastructure, linked end-to-end: data pipelines, feature engineering, modelling, validation and live execution.
  • AI agents. We design and deploy custom agentic solutions. Our agents work autonomously on research, DevOps and client communication.
  • Deep learning and LLMs. We build, train and fine-tune neural networks and large language models in PyTorch for domain-specific tasks, for example converting text data into numerical features.

Tissue simulation

Cell junctions in the outlined region are more contractile, forcing the cells to swap neighbours, so the whole tissue narrows along one axis and extends along the other.

This is a simulation of a fundamental process in development, in which a tissue restructures itself and changes shape using only forces generated within it. We model the cells of the embryo with a vertex model, which represents each cell as a polygon with a preferred area and a contractile perimeter. In the outlined region, the vertical junctions are more contractile, because of a genetic patterning of myosin II, the motor protein that makes cells contract. These junctions shrink until four cells meet at a point, and a new junction then grows at right angles to the old one. As cells exchange neighbours like this, the sheet narrows in one direction and lengthens in the other, as the germband (the strip of tissue that becomes the fly’s body segments) does when the embryo’s body axis extends. Drag the embryo to turn it, and click or tap a cell to remove it.

Cell simulation

A simulation of cell intercalation via junction contraction. Each cell has its own cortex, held to its neighbours by adhesions.

This is a small version of the Apposed-Cortex Adhesion Model, from my 2022 paper on how cells in a sheet exchange neighbours. Most models of epithelia draw the boundary between two cells as a single shared edge. In this one every cell has its own cortex, and the cortices of neighbouring cells are held together by adhesions that bind and unbind.

In the model, when the cortices along a junction contract, the junction shortens until four cells meet. The adhesions let the two cortices slip past each other, a new junction opens at right angles, and the cells have swapped neighbours. Click or tap a junction to make it the one that contracts.

What runs here is simplified so that it works in a browser and can repeat. The paper has the full model, and the code is public.
